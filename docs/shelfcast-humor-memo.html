01 The game already knows how to be funny — in two places

Reading through GameProgression.json, GameStakeholders.json, OfflineIntelArtifacts.json, and EndgameEpilogue.json turns up two distinct registers, not one flat "technical" voice.

Already working

Character introductions and epilogue text are dry, specific, and self-aware — closer to a novelist's voice than a spec sheet.

Hey, I'm model_monica. I train and tune models until the numbers stop embarrassing me, then I deploy them and keep watching the numbers anyway.GameStakeholders.json:87
A year on, nobody at Lindenmarkt calls it "the Shelfcast project" any more. They just call it the forecast, and they plan around it.EndgameEpilogue.json:4
Goes flat

The moment the negotiation mechanics kick in — challenge briefs, intel artifacts, in-scenario stakeholder positions — every character drops into the same "I will accept X if Y" sentence shape. The joke writing evaporates exactly where players spend most of their time.

I will accept losing the manual review step where I currently adjust each metric to fit the broader product strategy. In return, I need the automated output to align perfectly with our organizational roadmap.OfflineIntelArtifacts.json:23 — efficiency_emilia
A supplier has confirmed a bulk order for a middle aisle promotion that ships next week, leaving no room for model error. requirements_reuben insists the acceptance criteria must be fully governed...GameProgression.json:174

Playtesters noticed the seam, though not by name: one flagged an intel item as "odd," and a separate note asked to push the MLOps substance more into focus, not less — the complaint is that stakeholder back-and-forth currently crowds out the technical grounding, not that the game is too dry. That's a useful constraint: humor here should sharpen the technical specificity, not paper over it with generic jokes.

02 What the research says

Five findings worth designing around, pulled from serious-game, educational-humor, and UX-writing literature.

Springer RPTEL
2021

Humor's benefit is conditional: gains show up when jokes are content-relevant and reinforce the concept being taught. Humor used purely as a break between "the real content" produces emotional/social goodwill but doesn't move learning outcomes. → A joke about a stakeholder's KPI obsession should teach you something true about their incentives, not just be a joke.

arXiv 1903.03019
Phishing ed-game study

Casual players explicitly asked for humor and "an overall light-hearted theme" in a security-training game, and reported paying more attention because the premise struck them as silly. Humor is a stated player want here, not just a nice-to-have.

Edutopia / eLI

Humor's engagement effect transfers to non-humorous stretches of the same session — but comes with a cost: students given humorous material showed worse time management. Translated to Shelfcast: funnier intel artifacts might make players linger on flavor text at the expense of the actual planning task, which is worth watching in playtests.

UX Design Institute /
LogRocket, tone-of-voice

Funny-vs-serious is one tone dimension, and it has to be applied consistently — "if you decide to communicate seriously, you can't start throwing jokes randomly on various pages." This argues for humor as a designed voice per character/surface, not a sprinkle of one-liners.

EMS1 / Lexipol,
gallows humor

Gallows humor is a real coping mechanism in high-stress operational work (closely analogous to incident response) but reads as disrespectful the moment it's aimed at people rather than the situation, or shown to an audience outside the team. Directly relevant to rollout debriefs and postmortem text — see guardrails below.

03 A voice model, anchored on Good Omens

Good Omens is the clearest fit: Heaven and Hell run as two competing middle-management bureaucracies, complete with paperwork and org charts, while the actual apocalypse gets treated as a scheduling inconvenience. The joke is always the gap between how enormous the real stakes are and how small the characters' actual, in-the-room concerns stay — never a joke about whether Aziraphale or Crowley is competent. That maps onto Shelfcast almost exactly: a supply chain in real trouble, six stakeholders arguing about roadmaps. Two close relatives sharpen different parts of that same idea.

Monty Python

Dead Parrot, Ministry of Silly Walks

The Dead Parrot sketch gets its length from the shopkeeper cycling through euphemisms rather than admitting the parrot is dead. The Ministry of Silly Walks works because the civil servant treats an absurd bureaucratic function with total, unblinking sincerity. Both are institutional denial and procedure, delivered dead straight.

Principle: when something is broken, let a stakeholder or system deny it through escalating, increasingly strained euphemism instead of stating it.

Applied to Shelfcast
The widget isn't stale. It's historically stable. It's had the same value for three weeks because that value hasn't finished being current yet.

Douglas Adams

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy — the Vogons

The Vogons destroy Earth as routine highway paperwork, and are far more upset about being asked to skip a poetry reading than about the demolition itself. The bureaucratic process outweighs, in the characters' own minds, the catastrophe the process just caused.

Principle: when a process failure causes real damage, let the process continue on schedule, mildly annoyed at the inconvenience of the damage rather than alarmed by it.

Applied to Shelfcast
The nightly batch job dropped the northern stores' scan lines without incident, in the sense that nothing crashed and nobody was told.

The test that carries across both: who or what is this joke actually about? It should resolve to a stake, a process, or a euphemism — never to whether a specific stakeholder is competent.

04 Five techniques, mapped to our content types

Applying the voice model above to Shelfcast's writing surfaces: narration, endgame text, and the throughline across a playthrough. None of these touch functional UI copy — see the guardrail below on why that's deliberate.

1. Extend the deadpan narrator past the epilogue

narration & flavor text only

The epilogue's understated voice ("that second part took longer than the first") is the strongest writing in the game and currently fires once, at the end. Deadpan narration is cheap to apply broadly because it needs no joke construction, just restraint and specificity. Three concrete places to try it — flavor throughout, and note the turn-end example keeps the actual agreement count legible rather than hiding it behind the joke:

Scenario transition, flavor line
Loading next scenario...
Deadpan
Assembling the next scenario. It will contain a supplier, a deadline, and someone's strong opinion about both.
Turn-end summary — count stays legible
You reached agreement with 2 of 3 stakeholders this round.
Deadpan
Two stakeholders agreed. The third is still drafting a strongly worded email.
Narrated fact
The dashboard widget still displays the last successful value from three weeks ago, which no longer reflects current store performance.
Deadpan
The dashboard widget has shown the same number for three weeks straight. Nobody has corrected it, and it hasn't corrected itself.

One caveat on the scenario-transition example specifically: worth confirming a loading screen is actually seen in production before investing writing time there — it apparently barely shows up in-game as it stands, though that may just be an artifact of testing against localhost rather than a real deployment.

2. Let real stakes and petty focus share a scene

briefings, scenario intros

Good Omens' and the Vogons' shared move: state the real, large consequence plainly, then let the characters carry on caring about something much smaller, with no comment on the gap. The reader supplies the irony; the text stays completely straight.

Current — stated as one fact
A batch of till scan lines from the northern stores was silently dropped during last night's ingestion, causing the model to forecast zero demand for fresh produce.
Stakes + petty focus, same scene
The forecast now expects nobody, anywhere, to buy a single vegetable tomorrow. The scan-line issue is third on today's agenda, after room booking.

3. Gallows humor for bad-ending debriefs — carefully

endgame epilogue, incident text

The worst-ending epilogue already leans this way ("Nobody cancels Shelfcast. It keeps running, and the stores keep ignoring it..."). This register fits postmortem-style content well because it's real workplace humor — but the research is specific that it works when aimed at the situation, never at a specific stakeholder's competence or a real-feeling failure with human cost (e.g. a store running out of stock during a holiday). Keep it structural and absurd, never personal.

4. Anchor callbacks to persistent state, not to one object

cross-cutting callback

A running gag scripted around one specific object — say, a stale dashboard widget — across a fixed sequence of beats ending in an epilogue payoff doesn't hold up here. Challenges are picked by scheduler.py's next_challenge from whichever are currently eligible, gated by live graph state and a per-run seed, and each intel artifact only surfaces if its specific parent challenge_id got dealt. A callback scripted around one challenge's artifact can simply not appear in a given run, and even when it does, nothing currently reads "was the widget stale" as a fact the epilogue can pick up. Betting a whole thread on that is fragile, and betting the game's only running thread on one object is also just one joke repeated.

What's actually safe to build on: state that accumulates no matter which challenges fired — the fired_grudges counter, final_emotions per stakeholder, the computed pillar scores (stakeholder_relations, intel_accuracy, pipeline_health), and each graph component's own automation/governance state. Two separate systems already read state like this instead of a scripted sequence, and each of the three threads below fits one of them: the first two are epilogue-scoped — EpilogueFactory.beats_for() picks a beat whose declarative condition (e.g. intel_accuracy_below: 0.4) matches a facts dict built from that history, with a guaranteed fallback and stakeholder names filled in per run. The third is mid-game and component-scoped, through a different but analogous system detailed below it.

"The Ledger" — keyed to fired_grudges count
Grudge count crosses a threshold, anywhere, any run.
Narrator aside
Someone has started keeping a list. It has three entries so far.
"Coldest relationship" — keyed to final_emotions
Whichever stakeholder's mood is currently lowest, filled in like the epilogue already fills {st}.
Narrator aside
{st} has stopped replying with exclamation points. Someone is treating this as a leading indicator.
Governance drift — keyed to one component's own tier
Any component reaching the "automated" tier while its own governance stays at NONE — e.g. data.validation.
Narrator aside
Data validation runs on its own now. Nobody has signed off on what it's allowed to reject.

This last one doesn't need new plumbing — it already exists. Every component and edge in the graph (gameConfig/MlopsGraph.json: data.validation, deploy.serving, ops.rollback, and the rest) carries its own automation and governance state, collapsed into one display tier — broken / absent / manual / automated / governed (narrative_tier(), graph.py:90-103). MlopsStoryFragments.json already authors text per component-and-tier, with a generic fallback per tier and specific overrides keyed by component id. missing_specific_fragments() (story.py:48-58) lists every component/tier pair currently falling back to the generic text, but that's not where to start — an override for a gap might not be reached by any given run's component states. The generic per-tier templates, by contrast, are what fires whenever no specific override exists, which given how many gaps that helper reports is most of what players are actually reading today. Rewriting those five generic templates first touches confirmed, live text; filling in specific gaps after that is lower-priority polish.

A single stale-looking dashboard number is still fine as a one-off, local to its own artifact — it just shouldn't be asked to survive to the epilogue unless a fact for it gets added deliberately, which is a real implementation cost worth naming up front.

5. Give it a real turn: recursion, a moving goalpost, or a euphemism

challenge briefs, stakeholder documents

The rewrites in the next section that actually land (see "Why it's actually funny" on each) all use one of a small set of related mechanisms — not an accumulating dry tone, which is the thing that keeps reading as competent-but-not-funny. A joke needs a real turn: a reversal, a loop, or a reveal.

Recursive bureaucracy — a process needs its own meta-process before it can happen: a form to approve the form, an audit of the audit trail, an authorization to authorize the spend. This works because it's not an invented joke bolted onto the scenario — approval chains and audit requirements are real MLOps process, so pushing the recursion one step further is exaggeration of something true, not a non-sequitur.

Unresolved resolution — the negotiation reaches a full, formal, signed agreement, and the punchline is that nobody has actually checked whether the underlying problem got fixed. This one is close to free: it's exactly the gap this game exists to teach players to watch for, restated as a joke instead of as a lesson.

Retroactively-satisfiable criterion — a stakeholder's conditional trade-off ("I'll accept it if it clearly supports X") is stated plainly, and the joke is that the standard is vague enough to be declared met after the fact no matter what happens — a real corporate pathology (rewriting the goalposts to match the outcome), not an invented absurdity.

Institutional euphemism — the Monty Python device from the voice model, applied directly: a flat refusal gets dressed in escalating official-sounding language, and the joke is the visible labor of softening a "no" into policy language, not the refusal itself.

Before reaching for a different technique on a new brief, check whether one of these is already latent in its negotiation logic — a sign-off, an audit trail, a vague conditional trade-off, a hard refusal dressed as policy. Most Shelfcast challenges will have one.

05 Seven worked examples, pulled from the real files

Applying the techniques above to actual content instead of invented placeholders, with a plain check that the driver/boundary/trade-off logic survives each rewrite. The five briefs were deliberately assigned different techniques before writing, as a direct test of the anti-repetition guardrail below. The last two are intel artifacts, not briefs — see the note at the end of this section on why those were worth rewriting directly after all.

Challenge brief · recursive bureaucracy

The Committed Pallets — GameProgression.json:174

Original
A supplier has confirmed a bulk order for a middle aisle promotion that ships next week, leaving no room for model error. requirements_reuben insists the acceptance criteria must be fully governed to guarantee the allocation logic is auditable before the trucks leave. reliability_ruth argues the criteria are sufficient as manual checks because the physical stock is already committed and the team needs to ship the feature immediately to handle the live orders.
Rewrite
The order is locked, the trucks are booked for next week, and the model gets exactly zero do-overs. Reuben has requested that the acceptance criteria be fully governed and independently auditable — a request currently held up in the queue behind a separate form asking whether the audit trail may itself be audited. Ruth maintains this is unnecessary: the pallets are already committed, the orders are live, and manual checks got everyone through last Tuesday just fine. Nobody present today has been asked how they feel about the trucks leaving on time. Several people have strong feelings about the form.

Check: Reuben's governance/audit driver, Ruth's manual-checks-suffice boundary, the committed stock, and the live-orders trade-off are all intact. Why it's actually funny: the joke has a mechanism, not just a wry tone — a form to audit whether the audit trail can itself be audited, capped by the real deadline (the trucks) mattering to nobody in the room compared to the paperwork about the paperwork.

Challenge brief · unresolved resolution

The Midnight Deadline — GameProgression.json:845

Original
The nightly data job is failing to finish before the store managers open the ordering app. reliability_ruth demands an automated validation layer to catch bad data instantly so the morning window is never missed again. data_dave insists on keeping the process manual for now, arguing that the infrastructure costs are too high and the current manual checks are sufficient to manage the risk.
Rewrite
Store managers have submitted, via the appropriate channel, a Request for the Ordering App to Contain Data When Opened. Ruth supports the request and has attached a proposal for automated validation to catch bad data before the window closes again. Dave has attached a counter-memo noting that the infrastructure required to grant the request would itself require a separate request, and that in the meantime the manual checks are, per his last three memos, sufficient to manage the risk. All parties have signed. Nobody has opened the app since Tuesday to check.

Check: Ruth's automated-validation driver and both of Dave's stated reasons (infrastructure cost, manual checks are sufficient) survive intact. Why it's actually funny: three stacked turns — the mock-formal "Request," the infrastructure-needs-its-own-request recursion, and the real punchline, that a fully signed agreement was reached without anyone verifying the actual problem got fixed.

Challenge brief · literal scale collision, no metaphor needed

The Consent Gap — GameProgression.json:688

Original
A new model version requires loyalty basket data, but the deployment pipeline lacks the governance controls to verify consent. automation_alex wants to push the automated deployment forward immediately to meet the promotion deadline, while requirements_reuben insists on a manual review process to ensure legal compliance. automation_alex is open to adding a manual approval step if it keeps the pipeline moving, but requirements_reuben will not accept any automated path without full audit trails.
Rewrite
The deployment pipeline has no way to check whether loyalty programme customers ever consented to their basket data being used in Model v4, which is why the question is currently item 4 on the steering committee's agenda (between "office plant rotation" and "car park resurfacing"), and has been for three meetings running. Alex wants the automated deployment moved forward today regardless, since the promotion deadline doesn't wait for agenda items. Reuben will not let anything ship until item 4 is resolved, calling it a matter of legal compliance. Alex is open to adding a manual approval step if the meeting can end on time. Reuben will not accept any automated path without full audit trails, agenda or no agenda.

Check: the consent question is explicitly about customers, not the data itself; the pipeline's specific technical incapacity to verify that consent is preserved (not softened into generic "nobody checked"); Alex's deadline driver and manual-approval concession, and Reuben's audit-trail requirement, are all intact. Why it's actually funny: a real compliance risk sits, unremarked, between "office plant rotation" and "car park resurfacing" on a recurring agenda — no metaphor to decode, the juxtaposition of scale is the entire joke, stated flatly.

Challenge brief · retroactively-satisfiable criterion

The Summer Forecast Gap — GameProgression.json:533

Original
A sudden heatwave has skewed demand for seasonal goods, exposing that our KPI definitions do not account for weather driven spikes. model_monica wants to automate the KPI tracking to capture these shifts in real time, while efficiency_emilia prefers to keep the process manual to ensure every metric aligns with the broader business strategy. efficiency_emilia might accept an automated solution if it clearly supports the agreed business goals.
Rewrite
Seasonal demand just had a heatwave rewrite it overnight, and the KPI definitions, which do not currently believe in weather, were the last to find out. Monica's proposed fix is to automate the tracking so the numbers move in real time instead of next quarter. Emilia would rather it stayed manual, so every metric keeps pace with the broader business strategy by hand. She has floated one exception: an automated system that clearly supports the agreed business goals would be fine — a determination to be made later, by comparing the results to whichever goals turn out, in hindsight, to have been the agreed ones.

Check: the heatwave, the KPI blind spot on weather, Monica's real-time-automation driver, Emilia's manual/alignment boundary, and her conditional trade-off are all intact, close to verbatim. Why it's actually funny: it's a real, recognizable corporate pathology — a condition vague enough that it gets satisfied after the fact by declaring, once the results are in, which goals were "the agreed ones." The target is the sign-off process, not either stakeholder.

Challenge brief · institutional euphemism

The Cost Cliff — GameProgression.json:263

Original
The infrastructure bill for the monitoring stack has doubled, and efficiency_emilia has drawn a hard line: she will not fund automated drift monitoring, insisting it is a luxury the business cannot afford. reliability_ruth argues that without automated and governed drift checks, the team is flying blind during promotion weeks, and she refuses to accept a manual process as a substitute for reliable signal.
Rewrite
The monitoring stack's bill has doubled, which is the easy part of this meeting. The hard part is Emilia's official position on automated drift monitoring: "holding the line on discretionary reliability spend," a phrase that took three rounds of revision to sound less like no, attached to her original point that it is a luxury the business cannot afford. Ruth is not buying the rebrand. Without automated, governed checks she says the team is flying blind through every promotion week, and a manual process someone does when they remember is not, in her words, a signal. Nobody has blinked, and nobody has offered anything in trade.

Check: the doubled bill, Emilia's hard refusal and "luxury" framing, Ruth's flying-blind counter, her manual-isn't-a-substitute stance, and the flat deadlock (no trade-off either side) are all intact. Why it's actually funny: the joke targets the institutional labor of dressing up a refusal — "three rounds of revision to sound less like no" — not Emilia's character, and "Ruth is not buying the rebrand" pays it off without softening the deadlock.

Intel artifact · institutional euphemism, in his own voice

Reuben's governance memo — OfflineIntelArtifacts.json:267, Committed Pallets

Original
The supplier has confirmed the bulk order for the middle aisle promotion, and the trucks leave next week. Because the physical stock is already committed, there is no room for model error in the allocation logic. I am not willing to approve this release if the acceptance criteria remain manual. We must have the criteria fully governed to guarantee the logic is auditable before the goods ship. Manual checks are not sufficient for this scale of financial and operational risk. I will not sign off on the deployment until the governance framework is in place and the audit trail is verifiable. This is a hard requirement for my team. We cannot accept the liability of an unauditable automated decision when the inventory is already locked in with the supplier.
Rewrite
Quick note before the trucks roll. The order's confirmed, the stock is committed, and there is officially no room in this plan for the model to be wrong, which is a strange thing to type calmly into an email but here we are. I want to be direct about where I stand: I won't approve the release with manual acceptance criteria. In this building we have taken to calling unreviewed automated decisions "agile," and I'd like to retire that usage specifically for anything touching a fully loaded truck. The logic needs to be governed and auditable before goods ship. Manual checks are not sufficient for risk at this scale, financially or operationally, whatever term the deck uses for it. I will not sign off until the governance framework is in place and the audit trail is verifiable end to end. This is a hard requirement for my team. Once the trucks leave, the inventory is locked in with the supplier, and I am not the one who explains to anyone above me why an unauditable decision made the call. That liability is not mine to carry.

Check: every fact — deadline, no room for model error, Reuben's refusal, his audit/governance requirement, his liability stance — is intact and just as precisely stated as the original. Why it's actually funny: Reuben names a euphemism his own building uses and declines to use it here — "we have taken to calling unreviewed automated decisions 'agile'" — which is him noticing an institutional absurdity in his own dry voice, not the writer assigning him a quirk. Four of five candidates in this round got rejected for reaching for a banned "not X, it's Y" rhetorical crutch to force a beat; this is the one that got its joke a cleaner way.

Intel artifact · personification with a callback

Ruth's trade-off notes — OfflineIntelArtifacts.json:281, Committed Pallets

Original
I am willing to accept that the acceptance criteria remain manual checks for this specific release. We can skip the full governance audit trail for the allocation logic right now. In exchange for that reduced oversight, I need to ship the feature immediately so we can handle the live orders before the trucks leave. The physical stock is already committed to the supplier, and the window is closing. If we wait for the governed pipeline to be fully in place, we risk missing the delivery date and leaving the stores without the promised middle aisle stock. I am trading the long term auditability of this specific run for the immediate operational certainty of getting the pallets to the distribution centres on time.
Rewrite
The trucks do not know what an audit trail is, and I am not going to be the one to explain it to them before they leave. So: acceptance criteria manual for this release, full governance audit trail for the allocation logic skipped for now, feature shipped immediately so we can get the live orders handled before the trucks go. The stock is already committed to the supplier and the window is closing, which is a sentence that is true whether or not our pipeline is governed. Wait for the governed version and we miss the delivery date, and the stores are left without the middle aisle stock we told them was coming. What I am offering is simple: I will give up the long-term auditability of this one run, and take, in return, the pallets actually arriving at the distribution centres on time. The trucks get their certainty. The paperwork can wait its turn.

Check: the manual-checks concession, the skipped audit trail, the ship-now driver, the committed stock and closing window, and the explicit auditability-for-certainty trade are all intact and precise. Why it's actually funny: a real setup-and-payoff structure — "the trucks do not know what an audit trail is" opens it, "the trucks get their certainty, the paperwork can wait its turn" closes it — built entirely out of the actual trade-off, with no personality added to Ruth at all.

Both artifacts above belong to a real stakeholder's own written voice, not narration — which is exactly why the earlier instinct to leave them untouched was too cautious. The constraint that actually matters isn't "never touch testimony," it's the same one that applies everywhere else in this memo: every fact has to survive, and the joke has to come from the character noticing something true and absurd, never from the writer assigning them a trait. Tested against that bar with the same adversarial rigor as the briefs, both artifacts cleared it — and since intel artifacts render more often in the app than these scenario briefs do, they're arguably the higher-priority surface to rewrite, not the one to protect from rewriting.

06 How these seven were actually produced

A single drafting pass reliably lands on dry, competent understatement with no real joke in it — that's what happened the first time these briefs got rewritten. What produced the seven that actually work was a two-pass process, and it's worth adopting deliberately rather than trying to nail a joke in one attempt.

Pass one, write several candidates, not one. Given the voice model and hard constraints above, a writing pass generated five candidate rewrites per brief — explicitly told to take real swings, including some too broad to ship, rather than default to safe wry restatement.

Pass two, review adversarially. A separate pass was given the candidates with instructions to assume every one fails and make it prove otherwise: check every negotiation fact survived (reject on any drop, no partial credit), confirm the joke's target is a process or form and never a stakeholder's competence or tone, screen for AI-slop vocabulary, confirm it still reads as a comprehensible scenario setup — and, separately from all of that, judge whether it has an actual comedic mechanism rather than an accumulating dry tone.

On the first two briefs, two of ten candidates survived clean — three got rejected for quietly dropping a required fact to buy a better punchline, two more for the same understatement-with-no-turn problem in new sentences. That ratio is normal, not a failure. Whether this runs as two AI passes or as a writer handing a few options to a second, deliberately skeptical reader, the two roles are what catch what a single pass consistently misses.

Exclude, don't assign. The next three briefs were each given a specific target technique upfront, and on paper that worked — three assigned passes, three clean winners. But the actual best line of the batch, the Consent Gap's "haunted house with a Wi-Fi password," wasn't the assigned technique at all — it came from a candidate the writer was still free to try something else with, and it introduced a mechanism (a metaphor collision aimed at the object) that wasn't in the catalog before. Picking the destination in advance is efficient, but it's also a reliable way to never find a fifth technique. The version that keeps both the variety guardrail and the exploration is: hand the writer the list of shapes already in use so it can't accidentally repeat one, give it the full voice model, and let it find its own mechanism rather than aim at a chosen one — which is what the template below actually does.

Tag each shipped line with which technique it ended up using, whatever that turns out to be (a one-column note next to the content is enough, no tooling needed), and cap it — no technique repeats across two challenges in the same phase.

First-person testimony needs one more distinction, not fewer rules. Rewriting stakeholder documents directly (not just narration around them) worked cleanly once the reviewer was told explicitly to tell apart "the character notices something absurd about their own situation" from "the writer assigned them a trait" — the same rule 2 as everywhere else, just harder to apply in first person. It also surfaced a new failure mode worth watching for generally: four of five candidates in that round reached for the banned "not X, it's Y" reframe construction to manufacture a beat under pressure, which is apparently the easiest crutch available once a writer is stuck for a punchline — worth calling out explicitly as a thing to watch for, not just leaving it as one line item in a vocabulary list.

Writer pass

I'm writing comedic rewrites of dry scenario briefs for a "serious game" about MLOps. I need genuinely funny candidates, not just tightened prose — the failure mode to avoid is competent wry understatement with no real joke in it.

VOICE MODEL (don't deviate):
- Good Omens (Gaiman/Pratchett): real large stakes sit completely unremarked next to characters' small, petty, in-the-room focus. Never narrated or explained — just placed there.
- Monty Python (Dead Parrot / Ministry of Silly Walks): institutional denial via escalating euphemism; absurd bureaucratic functions treated with total unblinking sincerity.
- Douglas Adams (the Vogons): a bureaucratic process outweighs, in the characters' own minds, the catastrophe the process just caused — irritation about the paperwork inconvenience, not alarm about the disaster.

DEVICES ALREADY IN USE — don't repeat these exact shapes, but anything else, including something new, is fair game:
- Recursive bureaucracy: a process needs its own meta-process (a form to approve the form, an audit of the audit trail).
- Unresolved resolution: a full signed agreement is reached, and nobody checked whether the real problem got fixed.
- Retroactively-satisfiable criterion: a vague trade-off condition that can always be declared met after the fact.
- Institutional euphemism: a flat refusal dressed in escalating official-sounding language.
[ADD ANY NEWLY SHIPPED DEVICE HERE]

HARD CONSTRAINTS (a strict adversarial reviewer will reject anything that breaks these, no partial credit):
1. Every candidate MUST preserve every negotiation fact from the original. State what you preserved for each.
2. The joke's target must be a process, a form, a deadline, a mismatch of scale — NEVER a specific stakeholder's competence or personality. No caricature, no verbal tics, no mockery of an individual.
3. No AI-writing-tell vocabulary: delve, boasts, testament, tapestry, leverage, crucial, robust, seamless, underscore, "not X but Y" constructions, spaced em dashes, "load-bearing," "table stakes," "north star."
4. Must still function as a clear scenario setup for a player about to negotiate.
5. If a joke touches a technical, legal, or compliance term (consent, audit, governance, ownership, liability), it must preserve who or what that term actually refers to — don't personify or redirect it onto the wrong subject (data cannot consent; only the people it belongs to can).
6. Any metaphor or image used must be understandable on a single read. If it needs to be explained, cut it.

TASK: Write 5 genuinely funny candidate rewrites. Take real swings — some can be too broad, a reviewer will cut the weak ones. After each candidate, note in one line which facts it preserved, which mechanism it used, and confirm it passes rules 5 and 6.

Reviewer pass

You are an adversarial reviewer for comedy writing in a serious game about MLOps. Be genuinely harsh — assume every candidate probably fails, and make it prove otherwise. Do not be diplomatic.

HARD RULES (reject immediately, no partial credit, if violated):
1. Preserves every negotiation fact from the original (list them and check).
2. The joke's target is a process, a form, a deadline, a mismatch of scale — NEVER a specific stakeholder's competence, personality, or a verbal tic assigned to them. Watch closely for small "behavioral tics" given to a stakeholder (a quirky habit, an odd phrase repeated, a prop) — these are a rule-2 violation even when framed as "about the process," because they still individuate and lightly caricature a named person rather than the system.
3. No AI-writing-tell vocabulary/constructions: delve, boasts, testament, tapestry, leverage, crucial, robust, seamless, underscore, "not X but Y", spaced em dashes, "load-bearing," "table stakes," "north star."
4. Still functions as a comprehensible scenario setup for a player about to negotiate.
5. Preserves who or what any technical/legal/compliance term actually refers to — flag it if a joke personifies data, a system, or an object as the holder of something (consent, ownership, liability) that only a person can actually hold.
6. Any metaphor or image must be understandable on a single read with no decoding required — if you have to explain it yourself to justify it, fail it.

ADDITIONAL RULE 7: this project already has these jokes elsewhere: [PASTE THE SAME DEVICES-ALREADY-IN-USE LIST FROM THE WRITER PROMPT]. Mark down (don't auto-reject, but flag clearly) any candidate that repeats one of those exact shapes — a genuinely new device, even an unnamed one, is preferred over a strong instance of something already used twice.

07 Scaling this: a human-on-the-loop pipeline

Running the writer/reviewer pattern by hand doesn't extend past a handful of briefs. This is the light version for the rest of them, with exactly one required human checkpoint.

  1. Inventory. Pull every challenge brief with the clear two-stakeholder driver/boundary/trade-off shape the examples above share, tagged by phase and stakeholder pair — and pull the intel artifacts alongside them, not separately. Since artifacts render more often in the app than these briefs do, treat them as the higher-priority half of each batch, not an excluded category.
  2. Keep an exclusion ledger. A running list — challenge id, technique used, shipped line — seeded with the four devices already in use. Every batch reads from it and every shipped winner appends to it; this is what actually enforces the anti-repetition guardrail instead of relying on someone remembering to check.
  3. Batch by phase, not all at once. Two to four briefs per batch keeps any one writer/reviewer round from juggling too much variety, and keeps a bad batch cheap to redo.
  4. Writer pass, per brief. Fill the reusable template — title, original text, extracted facts, current exclusion ledger — and run it. Five candidates out.
  5. Adversarial reviewer pass, per brief. Same brief, plus the five candidates, into the reviewer template. Out comes a verdict table and either a single winner or an explicit "none of these are good enough."
  6. Human checkpoint — one gate, not per-candidate. A person reviews only the winners (and any flat rejections), not the fifteen-plus intermediate candidates. This is the step that would have caught the Consent Gap's data/customer mix-up before it shipped — the reviewer agent had no way to know loyalty data is a compliance object tied to real customers by default; a domain-aware human does. Approve, or send back with a one-line correction for another round.
  7. Ship, then update the ledger. The approved rewrite replaces the brief's description field; the technique used gets logged, closing the loop for the next batch's exclusion list.
  8. Playtest spot-check. The one thing that can't be automated at all: confirm the negotiation logic still reads clearly to an actual player.

08 Words to avoid

If we're hand-authoring more prose now, it's worth flagging the tells that make writing read as machine-generated — the exact opposite of the specific, authored voice this whole memo is arguing for. Wikipedia's editors compiled a public reference for this after cataloguing thousands of AI-flagged edits: Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing.

Overused vocabulary — words LLMs reach for far more than human writers do:

delve · boasts · testament · tapestry · vibrant · underscore(s) · crucial · robust · seamless · leverage · fostering · intricate · nuanced · realm · garnered · notably · multifaceted · streamline · showcase · highlight · pivotal · landscape · navigate · journey · unlock · elevate · resonate · ecosystem

Structural tells — patterns, not single words:

Grandiose, unearned significance ("stands as a testament to," "rich legacy," "plays a significant role"); the "not X, but Y" contrast that corrects a misconception nobody raised; spaced em dashes; "Certainly!" / "I hope this helps" / "in conclusion"; padded rule-of-three lists; opening a sentence with a participial phrase ("Nestled among...", "Boasting a...").

Bonus flag, not on Wikipedia's list but just as canned: tech-corporate clichés like "load-bearing," "table stakes," "north star," "circle back," "unpack this." Read new lines aloud; if one could sit unremarked in a LinkedIn post about a "transformation journey," cut it.

09 Guardrails

01

Content-relevant first. A joke that doesn't also carry a driver/boundary/trade-off fact is competing with the negotiation mechanic, not supporting it.

02

Never rewrite a functional hint — empty states, validation messages, tutorial prompts, anything a player needs in order to act — in a way that softens or buries the information itself. Humor belongs in narration and flavor text, not in the strings players depend on to understand what to do next.

03

Pick a register per surface and hold it: narration is deadpan, endgame text can go gallows. Don't mix registers within one line.

04

The joke's target is always a form, a ritual, or a mismatch of scale — never a specific stakeholder's competence, and never a scenario with real-feeling human cost (stockouts, layoffs implied by "efficiency" asks). Before shipping a line, ask who the joke is actually about.

05

Protect the technical signal. Playtesters already want the MLOps substance more foregrounded — every humor pass should be checked against "does this still teach the concept," not just "is this funny."

06

Run new lines through the words-to-avoid list above before shipping. A joke written in AI-slop vocabulary undercuts the entire point of doing this by hand.

07

Rewrite what's confirmed to render before writing anything new. A generic fallback template fires far more often than a fresh override for one rarely-hit component/tier gap, and the loading-screen example above shows the same trap from the other direction — content that's technically reachable but barely seen isn't worth writing first.

08

Don't let the reliable technique become the only technique — but steer by exclusion, not by destination. Hand a new writing pass the list of shapes already shipped so it can't accidentally repeat one, rather than assigning it a single target technique to aim at; picking the destination in advance is efficient but forecloses finding a genuinely new device. Tag each shipped line with whatever mechanism it ended up using, and keep no technique to more than one challenge per phase.

09

A joke that touches a technical or legal term (consent, audit, governance, liability, ownership) must keep the term's real subject intact — check who or what it actually refers to before personifying or redirecting it, since getting this wrong doesn't just weaken the joke, it misrepresents the exact concept the challenge is teaching. And any metaphor has to land in a single read; if it needs a footnote to work, it's not ready to ship.

10 Suggested pilot

Rather than rewriting every stakeholder and artifact at once, scope a small test:

  1. Rewrite the five generic per-tier templates in MlopsStoryFragments.json — they're what's actually rendering for players right now, ahead of any new specific override.
  2. Pick one state-anchored thread — "The Ledger" (fired_grudges) is the simplest, since the counter and its threshold already exist — and add a single narrator aside for it, gated the same way an epilogue beat is gated.
  3. Ship the Committed Pallets and Midnight Deadline brief rewrites and see how they read next to their unchanged intel artifacts.
  4. Confirm whether players ever actually see a loading/transition screen in production before writing more flavor text for it.
  5. Run it past the next playtest group and ask directly whether the negotiation logic still reads clearly — the research flags attention/time-management trade-offs as the thing to watch for.

Guidance for using AI Generated Text on Kaggle

Hello Kagglers,

With the rise of popular text generating AI models such as ChatGPT and Bard we think it’s important to share clear rules and principles for their use on the Kaggle forums. You may have seen that Stack Overflow banned the use of ChatGPT, here is a quote from them which highlights their rationale:

“The primary problem is that while the answers which ChatGPT produces have a high rate of being incorrect, they typically look like they might be good and the answers are very easy to produce.”

At Kaggle our discussion forum is primarily a place for our community to connect, learn from each other, and get answers to specific questions. When these AI tools are used to post content that is incorrect, is off topic, or is spammy in general - it harms our community and makes our discussion forums a less useful place for everyone.

As such we are publishing the following guidelines on the use of AI generated text on Kaggle:

1. Posts must be verified and meaningful. All content shared on Kaggle must comply with our community guidelines, and this continues to be true for generated content. If you are using generated text to answer a question, it is your responsibility to ensure it is meaningful, verifiably true, and on topic. In particular we will be taking action against any user who generates a high volume of meaningless posts using these tools.

2. All generated text must be labeled. Everyone deserves to know if they are reading a post written by a human or a computer. We are asking that all posts which use generated text must clearly state the tool they used to create the text at the start of the post. You should also include the prompt whenever that adds valuable context to the information you share. Labeling is required but is not sufficient to post generated text, you must also ensure your post is meaningful.

As this is a rapidly evolving space, we expect our policies and community norms around the use of generative AI models to also evolve. Please continue to share your feedback on the relationship you’d like to see our community establish with this technology.

Thank you!

2. Project Architecture & Detailed Feature Engineering  
     My project, codenamed "Satellite-V1," aims to predict the 5-day excess returns of the CSI 300 Index. It’s a Multi-Expert Ensemble system where each "Expert" observes the market through a different lens:Data Source: Historical OHLCV data since 2010 via the akshare API, synchronized with real-time minute-level snapshots.

Feature Breakdown:

Expert A (GRU - The Sequential Specialist):Input: 3D Tensor $(Batch, Time_Steps, Features)$.Features: Raw price action (Close, High, Low, Volume, Turnover) normalized using a rolling StandardScaler over a 30-day lookback window.Core Logic: A 2-layer GRU + Attention mechanism designed to capture long-short term memory effects amidst market noise.

Expert B (LGBM - The Statistical Indicator Specialist):Input: Flattened 2D Feature Matrix.Features: 80+ handcrafted technical indicators, including oscillators (RSI, KDJ), trend followers (MACD, DMA), volume-based metrics (OBV, WVAD), and lagged Log Returns.Core Logic: Leveraging Gradient Boosting Trees to find non-linear statistical edges in classical technical indicator combinations.

Expert C(CNN - The Morphological/Visual Specialist):Input: 2D Image-like Matrix $(6, 10, 10)$.Features: I reshaped 60 different derived metrics (MA crossovers, Bollinger Band widths, volatility clusters) into spatial 2D "market snapshots."Core Logic: Using Convolutional kernels to detect visual patterns, attempting to identify "Chartist" signals like Head-and-Shoulders or Bull-traps.

**Expert D **(Ridge - The Linear Baseline):Input: Basic statistical moments (Skewness, Kurtosis) and Moving Average distances.Core Logic: Acts as a "fail-safe" baseline to provide stable linear references and prevent non-linear models from "hallucinating" during extreme volatility.

The Commander (Ensemble Logic):I use a Ridge Regressor with L2 regularization to act as the "Commander." It takes the predictions from the four experts as inputs and learns dynamic weights. The final signal is executed after simulating transaction costs (commissions + stamp duty)

3. Three "Mysteries" Driving Me Crazy

#1: The "Severed Data Pipeline" (Proxy/API Issues)I frequently encounter ProxyError or RemoteDisconnected errors when using the akshare API for real-time inference, even without an active proxy. For those deploying live systems, how do you ensure the robustness of open-source data scraping? Any tips on caching or fallback mechanisms?

#2: The Overfitting ParadoxMy Expert A (GRU) shows textbook overfitting: the validation loss starts climbing after 50 epochs. However, in live simulations and backtests, this "overfitted" version significantly outperforms the "perfectly fit" versions (those with Early Stopping). I’ve strictly ruled out Look-ahead bias. Is "benign overfitting" a common phenomenon in noisy financial time series?

#3: The "Reverse Indicator" (Negative Weights)In the Commander’s weight distribution, Expert C (CNN) often receives a negative coefficient. This is bizarre—is the CNN essentially learning to be a contrarian indicator? Or does this imply severe Multicollinearity between my experts, forcing the Ridge regressor to flip signs to cancel out redundancy?

4. Call for Guidance

How should I diagnose multicollinearity between such diverse models (GRU vs. CNN vs. LGBM)?

Is mapping 1D financial indicators into 2D images for CNNs truly an effective denoising strategy, or just "feature-gluing"?
  As a freshman, is this architecture "over-engineered"?

Should I focus on deepening the models or go back to basics with Feature Engineering to find "Alpha"?

I’m eager to hear your thoughts. Every piece of advice is a massive "XP boost" for my learning journey! Thanks in advance!

It would really help if you could upload a simplified version of the project on Kaggle or at least share a small notebook that shows your data pipeline and validation setup. In trading problems the split logic and label construction often matter more than the model itself and people can give much better feedback when they can actually see that part.

For a freshman this is a very ambitious project and you are clearly thinking seriously about the problem. That said I do think the system is a bit over-engineered for the amount of stable signal you usually get from daily equity data. A lot of the strange behavior you are seeing can happen when the data pipeline is fragile or when the experts are highly correlated.

For the AKShare issue I would treat it as an engineering problem rather than a modeling problem. AKShare is fine for research but I would not trust it as a true live production feed. If you keep using it then add retries with backoff, keep a local cache, allow stale data fallback and if possible use a second source for verification. If your target is a 5 day excess return then minute level live data may be adding more operational risk than real predictive value.

For the GRU I would be careful about calling it benign overfitting too quickly. That can exist in theory but in practice the more common explanation is that the validation setup does not match the way the model is used in deployment. With a 5 day forward return target adjacent samples overlap in future returns so even if there is no obvious look ahead bias you can still get leakage-like effects unless you leave a proper time gap between train and validation. I would recheck that first. I would also select checkpoints based on trading metrics like IC, net return after costs and stability across rolling windows instead of only using validation loss.

The negative CNN weight is not automatically a bad sign. Ridge can assign negative coefficients and in an ensemble that often means the meta model is canceling out redundant signals from correlated experts. So yes the CNN could be acting like a contrarian indicator but the more likely explanation is multicollinearity. The best way to test that is to use out of fold predictions from each expert and then check pairwise correlations, VIF and how stable the commander weights are across rolling folds. If the CNN weight flips sign often then I would not trust it much.

I am also a bit skeptical about the CNN image setup. CNNs work best when the spatial layout has a real meaning. If the 2D arrangement is mostly an artificial reshape of unrelated indicators then it may be more like feature gluing than true denoising. In that case LGBM is often a stronger and more reliable baseline.

If I were you I would simplify first. Keep one strong tabular model, one sequence model and a simple ensemble. Then spend more time on validation design, target construction, transaction cost realism and feature quality. In financial ML that usually gives more real progress than adding extra architecture complexity.

Also I would not focus too much on the 60.17 percent win rate alone. Hit rate sounds nice but expectancy, drawdown, turnover and regime stability matter much more.

Best Practices for Understanding Dataset Before Modeling?

Hello all,

I am currently learning exploratory data analysis and noticed that understanding the dataset properly often feels more difficult than model building itself.

What steps do you usually follow before training your first model?
Do you prioritize visualization, statistics, or feature relationships first?

Any recommended workflow and Youtube resources would be very helpful for beginners like me.

@aiexplorer77 Before training your first model, focus on clearly understanding the dataset. Start by identifying the problem type and the target variable. Then examine the dataset structure using basic summaries such as shape, data types, missing values, duplicates, and descriptive statistics. Analyze the target distribution to check for imbalance or outliers, and explore individual features (both numeric and categorical) to understand their distributions and detect any unusual patterns.

Next, study the relationships between features and the target using correlations, group comparisons, and visualizations. This helps you identify important predictors and potential issues like skewness or multicollinearity. Only after understanding these patterns and cleaning the data should you begin modeling, as strong exploratory data analysis often improves results more than using complex algorithms.


Why Do Most Kaggle Models Fail in Real-World Deployment?

On Kaggle, we optimize for leaderboard score.
In production, we optimize for stability, interpretability, and monitoring.

Common gaps I’ve noticed:

Data leakage in notebooks

Overfitting to validation splits

No model monitoring strategy

No latency or memory constraints considered

Are we training ourselves to win competitions or to build deployable systems?

From my experience, the main issue is the data itself, there is a big difference between Kaggle Datasets and Real-World Datasets. We train the model on Kaggle Dataset, which is kind of biased, but the real-world data is more diverse, and the model performance drops significantly. @aryanmdev

How much Mathematics & Statistics is really required for Data Science?

Hi everyone,

I’m currently learning Data Science and trying to build a foundation. I often hear that mathematics and statistics are very important, but I’m confused about how deep I should go and which topics are actually essential for real-world data science work.

I’d love guidance from the community on:

What mathematics topics are most important? (Linear Algebra, Calculus, Probability, etc.)
Which statistics concepts are must-know for beginners to intermediate level?
What topics are frequently used in practical projects or competitions on Kaggle?
Are there any good YouTube channels or playlists you would recommend for learning these topics clearly?

I’m looking for a practical learning path rather than very theoretical depth.
I’d really appreciate your suggestions!

Look, as a Data Science student myself, I can tell you that you don't need to be a PhD mathematician, but you definitely need a solid grip on the basics to actually understand what's happening under the hood of your models. For Linear Algebra, focus on vectors, matrices, and eigenvalues because that's how data is represented, and for Calculus, you really just need to understand how derivatives work for things like Gradient Descent. Statistics is probably the most "daily" part of the job, make sure you're comfortable with probability distributions, hypothesis testing (P-values), and regression. On Kaggle, you’ll mostly use these for feature engineering and validating your models. For learning, check out 3Blue1Brown for the best visual math intuition or StatQuest with Josh Starmer,   he makes even the hardest stats concepts super easy to digest.

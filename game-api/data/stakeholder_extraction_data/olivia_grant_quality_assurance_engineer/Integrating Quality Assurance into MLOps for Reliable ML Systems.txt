
Abstract: 

Machine learning (ML) applications in critical sectors such as healthcare and finance face 
significant reliability concerns despite advancements in automated development and deployment 
pipelines. Conventional software quality assurance methods, which focus on code correctness, are 
insufficient for dynamic ML systems that are susceptible to data drift, bias, and adversarial attacks. 
This study addresses the fragmented systematic of quality assurance (QA) in machine learning 
operations (MLOps) by proposing an integrated framework. This framework embeds lifecycle-
wide QA modules spanning the data, model, and infrastructure layers into MLOps pipelines, 
coordinated via continuous integration and deployment (CI/CD). Improved reliability was 
demonstrated in a financial fraud detection case, as evidenced by fewer production incidents, rapid 
drift detection, increased fairness scores, and steady accuracy. Therefore, the case study validates 
that continuous and automated QA in MLOps significantly reduces operational risks and increases 
trust, thereby posing QA as an indispensable integrated service rather than a post-deployment 
afterthought for robust and ethical ML systems. We evaluate the framework on a financial-fraud 
workload over 90 days, contrasting a baseline pipeline without QA and a QA-integrated pipeline. 
The evaluation tracks fairness score (0–1), drift-detection latency, monthly production incident 
rate, accuracy stability, and data-validation error rate. 


INTRODUCTION 
ML is currently behind critical applications in health, transport, and finance, with concerns regarding its 
reliability, safety, and ethics. To address these issues, machine-learning operations (MLOps) merge data 
engineering, model development, deployment, and monitoring into automated pipelines, thereby improving 
scalability and efficiency [5]. However, MLOps do not guarantee quality assurance (QA). 

Conventional QA is well-grounded in code correctness through unit, integration, and regression tests. 
However, machine learning (ML) systems are nondeterministic in nature and evolve over time, facing 
threats such as data drift, bias, adversarial threats, and model aging [2]. Even the slightest drop in quality 
may have devastating financial and safety implications. Although MLOps have matured in terms of 
industrial adoption [10], QA remains ad hoc. Scholars have suggested that QA should transcend pre-release 
testing to be continuous and lifecycle-wide [1], [2]. 

ML reliability was improved by embedding QA modules throughout the entire lifecycle. Potential QA

LITERATURE REVIEW 
Prior work spans several themes. Technical debt analyses identify ML-specific risks such as entanglement, 
undeclared consumers, and data dependencies (Sculley et al.) [32] (see Table II, row 'Sculley et al. [32]'). 
Systematic surveys consolidate the testing landscape across properties, components, and workflows (Zhang 
et al.) [1] (see Table II, row 'Zhang et al. [1]'). A complementary practice-driven rubric, the ML Test Score, 
enumerates 28 concrete tests and monitors to raise production readiness (Breck et al.) [33] (see Table II, 
row 'Breck et al. [33]'). At the platform level, TFX operationalizes production pipelines and data validation 
at the Google scale (Baylor et al.; Polyzotis et al.) [10], [38] (see Table II, rows 'Baylor et al. [10]' and 
'Polyzotis et al. [38]'). In data dynamics, concepts and dataset shifts are extensively covered (Gama et al.; 
Suárez-Cetrulo et al.) [26], [34] (see Table II, rows 'Gama et al. [34]' and 'Suárez-Cetrulo et al. [26]'), and 
prospective clinical evidence quantifies performance erosion under temporal and infrastructural shifts 
(Mitchell et al.) [3]. Broad fairness surveys and reporting frameworks (Mehrabi et al.; Caton and Haas; 
Mitchell et al.) [3], [35], [36] (see Table II, rows 'Mehrabi et al. [35]' and 'Caton and Haas [36]') emphasize 
metrics and transparency. Our contribution is an end-to-end QA orchestration that coordinates these checks 
inside the CI/CD, links them to governance controls (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 5338), and demonstrates 
measurable reliability and fairness gains (Table III). 

The paradigm that operationalizes ML Systems with automation and scalability as the essential features is 
known as MLOps [5], [9], [10]. Deployment works, however, studies rarely consider quality assurance 
throughout the entire lifecycle. Traditional software QA checks whether a code is written according to the 
specifications by running unit, integration, and regression tests [2]. However, these testing methods are 
unsuitable for ML, and data quality, fairness, and robustness must be ensured. 

Several studies have focused on the specific QA aspects. Renggli et al. [6] focused on data quality, whereas 
Soni et al. [20] empirically demonstrated its direct link with the prediction performance. Ashmore et al. 
[13] surveyed safety and reliability interventions throughout the lifecycle, whereas Tu et al. [19] proposed

Fairness metrics. Following foundational notions of individual and group fairness - Fairness Through 
Awareness [29], Equalized Odds [30], and Disparate Impact [31]–we report the raw DPD/EOD/SRR and 
an interpretable composite score. The sensitivity (±10%) shows that our conclusions are robust to weight 
choice. 

Risk and compliance baselines. We align our QA controls with the recognized frameworks NIST AI RMF 
1.0 [27] and ISO/IEC 5338:2023 [28], mapping our monitors and approvals to governance functions. This 
improves auditability and reproducibility without disclosing proprietary artifacts. 

Testing ML systems. Industry reports highlight the gaps between traditional software testing and ML-
specific validation [4]. We complement this line of work with ablations of QA modules and lifecycle-wide 
risk metrics, rather than single-point accuracy. 

Monitoring and observability. A multivocal review of monitoring ML systems and empirical studies of 
testing in the wild [25] show fragmented practices, especially for cross-cutting signals (drift, incidents, and 
fairness). Our framework unifies these signals into operational triggers (retrain, rollback, and human-in-
the-loop) with clearly defined debounce windows and SLO-style thresholds. 

MLOps Architecture and Governance. Recent surveys have consolidated the MLOps landscape, yet stop 
short of prescribing QA-centric interfaces and escalation paths. Diaz-De-Arcaya et al. [23] synthesized 
challenges and a roadmap across MLOps/AIOps, whereas Warnett et al. [24] analyzed the understandability 
of MLOps system architectures. Complementary to this, we specify QA modules (data QA, model QA, and 
infra QA) as first-class services with contract-level thresholds and roles. 

BACKGROUND: QA IN THE ML LIFECYCLE 
The ML lifecycle involves data acquisition, model development, deployment, and continuous monitoring. 
Unlike traditional software, ML systems require ongoing QA owing to their dynamic environments. The 
key QA concerns include the following: 

1. Data ingestion - integrity, anomaly detection, bias auditing 
2. Model training - reproducibility, robustness, adversarial testing 
3. Evaluation - accuracy, calibration, interpretability, fairness 
4. Deployment - pipeline stability, security 
5. Production - Drift detection, anomaly detection, accuracy monitoring 
6. Retraining - feedback verification and error prevention 

Ongoing QA is critical for minimizing risks such as data drift, concept drift, bias, and model aging 
throughout the ML lifecycle, as shown in Fig. 2.  
This landscape motivates treating QA not as a one-time gate but as a continuous, automated service 
embedded throughout the ML lifecycle.

IV. MLOPS PIPELINES QA INTEGRATION FRAMEWORK 
The weaknesses identified in previous methods indicate the necessity for an end-to-end integrated QA 
system throughout the ML lifecycle. This section presents the proposed architecture, which includes the 
data, models, and infrastructure QA modules in a holistic MLOps pipeline. Unlike traditional QA, which is 
treated as a checkpoint task, this framework considers QA as an automated and continuous service. 

A. Architectural Overview 

The structure is designed in four layers: 

• Data QA - data quality, bias, and drift. 

• Model QA checks pre- and post-deployment performance, fairness, robustness, and interpretability. 

• Infrastructure QA: Ensures code and system component reproducibility and reliability. 

• Orchestration and Automation: Orchestrates QA modules using CI/CD-enabled pipelines. 

QA modules are integrated into each MLOps stage with automated retraining triggers, as shown in Fig. 3.

B. Data Quality Assurance 

Data is the foundation for ML to be performed, and the QA process is a necessary step. The framework 
supports schema validation, anomaly detection, and automated profiling [8]. Bias detection components 
look for imbalances in the training data, thereby reducing ethical risks [4], [16]. Following Bayram et al. 
[18], the system has a data-quality scoring module that constantly measures the production streams of data. 
These checks are incorporated into the pipeline such that failure in the data stops downstream operations 
until failure is addressed to avoid error propagation. 

C. Model Quality Assurance 

Pre-deployment QA involves accuracy metrics, stress testing for robustness, fairness, and interpretability 
[12], [14], and reproducibility tests [2]. Post-deployment QA includes monitoring the concept drift, 
anomalies, and performance decay [20], [21] with automatic retraining triggers. Banerjee et al. [21] 
proposed observability modules for real-time health data. 

 D. Code and Infrastructure QA 

In addition to data and models, the reliability of the infrastructure is important. Code artifacts have version 
control, unit tests, and integration tests, and are deployed via CI/CD [22]. The infrastructure-as-code was 
tested for reproducibility and compliance with organizational standards. Dong et al. [15] argued that safety 
arguments must be provided for ML components in critical systems, which can be accomplished through 
automated compliance tests and security vulnerability scanning.

E. Orchestration and Automation 

In MLOps pipelines, QA modules are managed by workflow coordinators (e.g., Kubeflow, MLflow, and 
Airflow) [7]. QA checkpoints are embedded in automated steps, focusing on schema and drift detection. 
Any failed QA can trigger an alarm or corrective action. Similarly, this automates reliability and quality 
processes. In our case study, integrating QA checkpoints did not materially increase end-to-end 
training/deployment time; however, the overhead depends on data volume and test configuration. In 
summary, this allows MLOps to function as a reliability paradigm that addresses technical and ethical risks 
throughout the lifecycle and provides managers with a means to ensure that ML systems are trustworthy, 
maintainable, and compliant.

CASE STUDY EVALUATION  
A case study is conducted to demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed QA-integrated MLOps pipeline. We 
selected a financial fraud detection system as a case study because high-stakes ML applications in the 
finance domain are susceptible to critical financial and ethical risks due to errors or bias. 

A. Case Study Background  

The baseline fraud-detection pipeline implements a typical MLOps workflow with limited post-deployment 
monitoring. It handles millions of transactions daily, and its performance degrades over time owing to data 
drift, with challenges in anomaly detection and potential fairness issues across different customer segments. 
The lack of systematic checks makes this a suitable case for applying the QA integration methodology. 

B. Implementation Steps  

The QA-enriched pipeline added the following QA modules:

• Data QA – This includes schema validation, anomaly detection, and fairness auditing of the input 
transaction data. 

• Model QA – Performed robustness and interpretability tests pre-deployment and integrated drift 
detection with automated model retraining alerts post-deployment. 

• Infrastructure QA – Ensured reproducibility of infrastructure-as-code setups, performed 
vulnerability scanning, and conducted CI/CD compliance tests. 

• Orchestration – Leveraged CI/CD pipelines for automating and orchestrating QA-enriched 
processes without compromising performance. 

Data and Ethics. The fraud detection data used for evaluation consisted of fully anonymized, contract-
restricted transaction records. All analyses were conducted under data handling agreements and internal 
policies, and no personally identifiable information was processed outside the secured environment. The 
reported aggregates were non-reversible. 

C. Evaluation Methodology  

We compared the performance of two pipelines for 90 days: 

• Baseline pipeline – A standard MLOps workflow without QA integration. 

• QA-integrated pipeline – The proposed QA-augmented MLOps framework.  

 

Metrics for evaluation included:  

• Error rate in data validation  

• Latency in drift detection  

• Monthly production incident rate  

• Fairness score (0–1 scale, higher is better)  

• Accuracy variance over 90 days  

Operational definitions: Data quality uses a standard schema, range, domain, and cross-field checks on 
hourly batches. Drift is monitored with the Population Stability Index (PSI) over fixed bins and a two-
sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test on prediction scores, with significant drift at PSI ≥ 0.25 or KS p < 
0.01. Fairness was evaluated weekly via demographic parity difference (DPD) and equal opportunity 
difference (EOD) with policy thresholds of DPD ≤ 0.10, EOD ≤ 0.10, and selection-rate ratio ≥ 0.80. 
Accuracy stability refers to the rolling band of daily accuracy from delayed labels, whereas the 30-day 
mean incident rate measures production reliability. 

Reproducibility: Methods and windows are specified in the text, and standard tooling is used; therefore, 
results can be reproduced without releasing configs or code. 

Composite Fairness Score: We report a single 0-1 fairness score as an average of the normalized 
components: fairness score = 1 - (|DPD|/0.10)*w1 - (|EOD|/0.10)*w2 - max(0, (0.80 - SRR )/0.80)*w3, 
where w1 = w2 = w3 = 1/3. The scores are clipped to [0, 1]. In addition, we tracked the raw DPD, EOD, 
and selection rate ratio (SRR) for transparency.

To aid replication without releasing internal configurations, we provided parameter ranges for PSI (bin 
count 10-20), KS (two-sample, α=0.01), and drift alert debounce (30-60 minutes); implementation can rely 
on standard libraries. 

D. Summary of Findings  

In Table III, we summarize the quantitative improvements: 

 

• The QA-integrated pipeline detected 29 percentage points more data validation errors, thus 
improving error resilience. 

• QA-integrated pipeline reduced drift detection latency by 35%. 

• QA-integrated pipeline had a 43% lower monthly production incident rate. 

• The QA-integrated pipeline had a 17% higher fairness score, which enhanced the bias mitigation. 

• The QA-integrated pipeline had a more stable accuracy, with a variance of ±3% compared to ±7% 
for the baseline. 

 

E. Key Takeaways  

These metrics demonstrated the practical impact of the QA-augmented MLOps pipeline. This shows that 
comprehensive QA checks not only mitigate pre-existing risks, but can also proactively prevent issues, 
enhancing both model performance and system reliability. This serves as an empirical endorsement of the 
framework, confirming that our design successfully incorporates scalability and rigour. 

 

DISCUSSION 
The case study findings indicate that QA, as an ingredient of the MLOps pipelines, yields a real increase in 
the reliability, responsiveness, and stability of the models. The pipeline reduces data-related failures, 
accelerates drift detection, and ensures performance stability by using QA modules at each lifecycle phase. 
These findings agree with those reported by Renggli et al. [6], who discovered that data quality is key to 
model reliability and is compatible with the emphasis on observability that Banerjee et al. [21] made central 
to strong ML systems. 

The framework advances ethical AI behavior by screening algorithms for bias and increasing fairness 
scores. It enhances transparency and trust but also has its limitations: it is expensive to compute, its use is 
limited to a few specific cases, and professionals need to supervise it. Notwithstanding these difficulties, 
QA implementation in MLOps pipelines is a potential solution for successful, secure, and ethical ML 
deployment [4, 12, 17]. 

In practice, the solution is costly to compute, validated in a single case, and dependent on expert 
supervision.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

The absent component of the current MLOps practices covered in this work was the lack of systematized, 
lifecycle-spanning QA. Compared to traditional QA models that merely test for code correctness, an ML-
applied model demands data quality, model robustness, fairness, and interpretability guarantees, in addition 
to those related to infrastructure reliability. To resolve this problem, we have suggested a QA integration 
model that integrates validation and monitoring modules at every step of the ML lifecycle, orchestrated by 
pipelines enabled by CI/CD. A financial fraud detection case study demonstrated the practical advantages 
of this framework, such as a reduction in the number of production incidents, quicker drift detection, better 
fairness measures, and greater accuracy and stability. These findings substantiate the claim that QA, as an 
automated service that should be treated as a continuous process, enhances technical reliability and ethical 
trustworthiness in the deployment of ML systems. 

The next phase of research should apply the framework to various domains, test its scaling under large-
scale industrial scenarios, and establish common benchmarks for QA in MLOps. Further research on the 
cost–benefit trade-offs of ongoing QA must be conducted before ubiquitous adoption.
